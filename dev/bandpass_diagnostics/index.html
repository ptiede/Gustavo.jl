Bandpass diagnostics

Once a bandpass is divided out, every spectrum should be the source alone, flat in amplitude and phase. These functions measure how far corrected data are from that, compare bandpass models, and find where a station's bandpass changed between scans. They report what is left, a residual delay included, rather than fitting anything out of it.

  • tss: how far each corrected spectrum is from flat.
  • chi2: the χ² of data against a model.
  • normalized_residuals: each spectrum over its mean along an axis.
  • step_tests: an F test for a change in one station's bandpass at each gap between its scans.

Weights

All three read the weights as the inverse variance of each real component of the visibilities. A χ²/dof of 1 then means a perfect fit. If the weights are right only up to a factor per baseline, weight_scales measures that factor from the scatter between consecutive samples and scale_weights! applies it:

foreach(ms -> scale_weights!(ms, weight_scales(ms)), values(ps))

A comparison between two models fitted to the same data does not need this: both χ² values scale with the weights, and the ratios below cancel the scale.

Input

Each function takes a processing set or a single Measurement Set. It lays the data out as baseline_spectra does, over (Ti, AntennaPair, FeedPair, Frequency) with the channels of every spectral window on one Frequency axis, and skips flagged samples. A baseline_spectra result works too, which saves laying the data out again for each call. Results keep those dimensions, less the ones reduced.

A bandpass error is the same in every integration of a scan, while the noise averages down, so scan averages show it most clearly: pass average(ps, ByScan()). Unaveraged data give one value per integration.

Goodness of fit

Both functions return a DimStack with a degrees-of-freedom layer dof.

tss is the weighted sum of squares of each cut along dims about its own weighted mean, one value per cut. Along Frequency, the default, it is the χ² of a source that is constant across the band, the model a corrected spectrum should satisfy, with that constant fitted to each spectrum. Totals are sums:

using XRadio: average, ByScan
q = tss(average(calibrate(sol, ps), ByScan()))[FeedPair = At([(1, 1), (2, 2)])]
sum(q.tss) / sum(q.dof)                              # the whole set
sum(q.tss; dims = Ti) ./ sum(q.dof; dims = Ti)       # per baseline and feed pair
mine = [p for p in lookup(q.tss, AntennaPair) if "S1" in p]
sum(q.tss[AntennaPair = At(mine)]) / sum(q.dof[AntennaPair = At(mine)])   # one station

With dims = Ti, each channel is taken about its mean over time instead. On unaveraged data, tss(ms; dims = Ti) tests whether a Measurement Set's channels hold steady through its scan.

A value per degree of freedom well above 1 says the correction left structure across the band. A residual delay is such structure: a phase slope that, if it changes from scan to scan, is a missing delay term in the gain model rather than anything a bandpass shape can follow. normalized_residuals shows which it is.

chi2 compares the data with any model of the visibilities, a DimArray over some of the data's dimensions, which is used for every sample along the dimensions it lacks. Here dims only names what is summed:

chi2(average(ps, ByScan()), model; dims = (Frequency, Ti))   # Σ w |v - model|² per baseline and feed pair

Every usable sample counts 2 degrees of freedom. The model's own free parameters are not charged; subtract them from dof.

To compare a model B with a model A that adds Δp parameters (a break, say), fit both to the same data and form

\[F = \frac{(\chi^2_B - \chi^2_A)/\Delta p}{\chi^2_A/\nu_A}.\]

Here χ² is each model's tss on the data it corrected, or its chi2 against a common model. If A's residuals were noise, F would follow F(Δp, νA), whose upper quantiles are close to 1 for large `νA`. Residuals of real data are rarely noise. They carry structure that changes from scan to scan, and nearly any extra parameters absorb some of it. So the quantile alone accepts almost any addition. Read F as the factor by which the addition beats the average misfit, and require a large one.

Residuals

normalized_residuals divides every cut of the data along an axis by its weighted mean along that axis, and gives the variance of each component. Along Frequency, the default, this removes each spectrum's phase, amplitude scale and band-mean source visibility. What is left is the structure across the band that the correction did not remove. That includes a residual delay, seen as a phase slope, which is the sign of a missing delay term:

using XRadio: average, ByScan
res = normalized_residuals(average(ps, ByScan()))
k = findfirst(==("SRC_3"), res.scan_name)
angle.(res.r[Ti = k, FeedPair = At((1, 1))])         # residual phase per baseline

Scan averages, shorter averages and unaveraged data all work. Along Ti, each channel is taken about its mean over time instead:

normalized_residuals(ms; dims = Ti)

Scan averages raise the signal-to-noise each spectrum is normalized with, and lose nothing for a bandpass that holds over a scan. They assume the phase is coherent through the scan. A phase common to all channels cancels in the ratio, but a delay drifting within the scan decorrelates the outer channels more than the inner ones.

Step tests

step_tests asks, for each gap between two of a station's consecutive scans, whether the residual spectra on the station's baselines are better described by one mean spectrum on each side of the gap than by one overall:

intervals = scan_intervals(ps)
rows = step_tests(res, intervals, "S1")
rows[argmax([r.F for r in rows])]          # the gap with the strongest step

F is normalized by the scatter of the scans about the two means, so scatter between scans that is not a step leaves F near 1. A residual delay that changes from scan to scan is such scatter, and lowers F at every gap. A delay that changes across a gap is a step. Reading a station's F against time:

  • A step peaks. The F of a step is largest at the gap where it happened. It falls off as the split moves away and mixes the two bandpasses on one side. Gaps only minutes from the step move one short scan to the other side and read almost the same F. So compare the peak with gaps well away from it, not with its neighbors.
  • A drift plateaus. A bandpass changing slowly through a track gives similar F at many gaps, and its maximum moves when the data change a little. Every cut through a drift is significant, but no single gap is where it happened.
  • A step leaks through shared baselines. A step at station S1 shows on every baseline to S1, including S1–S2. It therefore raises the F of S2, most when S1–S2 is S2's most sensitive baseline. Fitting the break to each candidate in turn tells them apart: the break at the station that owns the step removes more χ².
  • A station step is common to the station's baselines. A bandpass multiplies every baseline to its station alike, so a real change at S1 appears on all of S1's sensitive baselines at the same gap. Testing each baseline alone shows whether it does:
using DimensionalData: lookup, At
for p in lookup(res.r, AntennaPair)
    "S1" in p || continue
    rows = step_tests(res[AntennaPair(At([p]))], intervals, "S1")
    println(p, " ", maximum(r -> r.F, rows))
end

A change that shows on one baseline, or at different times on different baselines, is not a station bandpass change. It does not close. A station term can only move it onto the station's other baselines.
