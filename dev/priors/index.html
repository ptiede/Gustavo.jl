Fitting under priors

A component's prior (see Specifying gain models) relates its parameter values along one axis: time for the adhoc phase, frequency for the bandpass. This page derives how a solver fits a track of values under such a prior: the maximum a posteriori (MAP) values, the Kalman filter and Rauch–Tung–Striebel (RTS) smoother that compute them for the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) and random-walk priors, the type-II MAP estimate of the prior's own hyperparameters, and the choice of a phase track's 2π branches. The last section says where each solver uses them.

One track

A solver reduces its data to real tracks: a phase (on branches chosen as in Phase tracks and their 2π branches) or a log-amplitude per sample, with an inverse-variance weight. Write one track as

\[y_k = L + f_k + \varepsilon_k, \qquad \varepsilon_k \sim \mathcal N(0, r_k), \quad r_k = 1/w_k,\]

for samples $k = 1, \dots, n$ at coordinates $x_1 < \dots < x_n$ (seconds or Hz). $f$ is the structure the prior describes, $L$ a level the prior says nothing about, and $\varepsilon$ the measurement noise. A sample whose value is not finite, or whose weight is not positive and finite, carries no data. The spacing $x_{k+1} - x_k$ need not be uniform.

For the bandpass, a track is one spectral window of one (station, feed, time segment), sampled at the frequency segments' mean channel frequencies, and $L$ is the level component's value for that window; a phase may also carry a delay, which adds $2\pi\tau(x_k - f_0)$ to $L$ (see The mean). For the adhoc phase, a track is one (station, feed node) over the accumulation periods (APs) of one scan, and $L$ is the per-scan constant, a gauge that the adhoc solver removes afterwards (see solve_adhoc_phasing).

The OU prior and its MAP values

Under an OUPrior(; scale = τ, σ), $f$ is a zero-mean Gaussian process with covariance

\[K_{jk} = \sigma^2 \exp\!\big(-|x_j - x_k| / \tau\big).\]

With $L$ fixed, the posterior of $f$ is Gaussian, so its MAP value is also its posterior mean:

\[\hat f = K H^\top \big(H K H^\top + R\big)^{-1} (y - L),\]

where $H$ selects the samples with data and $R = \mathrm{diag}(r_k)$. At samples without data this interpolates, or extrapolates toward zero, from the covariance. Solved densely this costs $O(n^3)$; the OU process has a structure that brings it to $O(n)$.

The OU process is Markov

The exponential covariance is that of a first-order autoregression with coefficients that depend on the spacing. With $\Delta_k = x_k - x_{k-1}$,

\[f_1 \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2), \qquad f_k = a_k f_{k-1} + \eta_k, \quad a_k = e^{-\Delta_k/\tau}, \quad \eta_k \sim \mathcal N\!\big(0,\, q_k\big), \quad q_k = \sigma^2 (1 - a_k^2)\]

(ou_step). Every $f_k$ then has variance $\sigma^2$, and $\mathrm{cov}(f_j, f_k) = \sigma^2 \prod a = \sigma^2 e^{-|x_j - x_k|/\tau}$, the covariance above. A long gap makes $a_k$ small, so the process forgets across it; a short one keeps neighboring values close. This state-space form of the exponential (Matérn-1/2) covariance is standard; see Särkkä and Solin, Applied Stochastic Differential Equations (2019).

Kalman filter

Both priors on this page are linear-Gaussian state-space models: a small state $s_k$ per sample, whose first entry is $f_k$, evolving over each step as

\[s_k = A_k s_{k-1} + \eta_k, \qquad \eta_k \sim \mathcal N(0, Q_k).\]

For the OU prior the state is $f_k$ alone, with $A_k = a_k$ and $Q_k = q_k$ (OUModel); the random-walk prior below has $m$ states. One filter and smoother fit either.

The forward pass (kalman_filter) carries the mean $\mu_k$ and covariance $P_k$ of $s_k$ given samples $1, \dots, k$. Each step predicts

\[\mu_k^- = A_k \mu_{k-1}, \qquad P_k^- = A_k P_{k-1} A_k^\top + Q_k,\]

starting from the prior's start (for OU, $\mu_1^- = 0$, $P_1^- = \sigma^2$), and, if sample $k$ carries data, updates with the innovation $v_k$ and its variance $S_k$:

\[v_k = y_k - L - (\mu_k^-)_1, \quad S_k = (P_k^-)_{11} + r_k, \quad K_k = P_k^- e_1 / S_k, \quad \mu_k = \mu_k^- + K_k v_k, \quad P_k = P_k^- - K_k S_k K_k^\top,\]

with $e_1$ the first unit vector; $P_k$ is computed in Joseph form so it stays symmetric and positive definite. A sample without data is predicted through: $\mu_k = \mu_k^-$, $P_k = P_k^-$.

RTS smoother

The backward pass (rts_smooth) conditions each state on the samples after it as well:

\[G_k = P_k A_{k+1}^\top (P_{k+1}^-)^{-1}, \qquad \hat s_k = \mu_k + G_k \big(\hat s_{k+1} - \mu_{k+1}^-\big),\]

starting from $\hat s_n = \mu_n$. The first entry of $\hat s_k$ equals the dense posterior mean above exactly, in $O(n)$ time and memory, for any spacing (smooth_track, smooth_ou_track).

The marginal likelihood

The innovations are independent, so the forward pass also gives the likelihood of the data with $f$ integrated out:

\[\log p(y \mid \tau, \sigma, L) = -\frac12 \sum_{k \text{ with data}} \Big[\log(2\pi S_k) + \frac{v_k^2}{S_k}\Big].\]

This is what the hyperparameters are estimated from.

The mean

The level is the simplest case of a mean that is linear in unknown coefficients, $L_k = h_k^\top \beta$: a level is one coefficient with $h_k = 1$, and a level plus a delay two, with $h_k = (1,\, 2\pi(x_k - f_0))$ and $\beta = (L, \tau)$. The filter is linear in its observations, so the innovations of $y - H\beta$ are $v^y_k - (v^H_k)^\top \beta$, where $v^y$ are the innovations of $y$ and $v^H$ those of each regressor, all run with the same gains (_mean_sums). With

\[b = \sum_k \frac{v^y_k\, v^H_k}{S_k}, \qquad C = \sum_k \frac{v^H_k (v^H_k)^\top}{S_k},\]

the likelihood is quadratic in $\beta$ and maximized at the generalized least squares mean $\hat\beta = C^{-1} b$. Tracks that share a coefficient (the spectral windows of one level segment, or every window a delay spans) sum their $b$ and $C$ into it.

$\beta$ is not known when the hyperparameters are estimated, and plugging in $\hat\beta$ would bias $\sigma$ low on short tracks, since the mean absorbs part of the structure. Instead $\beta$ is integrated out under a flat prior (the restricted likelihood), which adds

\[\tfrac12 b^\top C^{-1} b - \tfrac12 \log\det C\]

to the log likelihood of the zero-mean tracks, up to a constant; for a level alone this is $b^2/2c - \tfrac12\log c$ per level. Given the hyperparameters, the fit is then $H\hat\beta$ plus the MAP of $y - H\hat\beta$.

Type-II MAP of the hyperparameters

Each of scale and σ in an OUPrior is either a number, held fixed, or a hyperprior density (any object implementing DensityInterface's logdensityof, such as a Distributions.jl distribution). A hyperprior is resolved by maximizing the marginal likelihood times the hyperprior density, searched over $(\log\tau, \log\sigma)$:

\[\max_{\tau, \sigma}\; \log p(y \mid \tau, \sigma) + \log \pi_\tau(\tau) + \log \pi_\sigma(\sigma) + \log\tau + \log\sigma,\]

where $p(y \mid \tau, \sigma)$ is the restricted likelihood above when the track has a mean, the zero-mean one otherwise, summed over every track the solver pools, and $\log\tau + \log\sigma$ is the Jacobian of the change to logarithmic coordinates. This is type-II MAP (also called empirical Bayes with a hyperprior): the values are integrated out, the hyperparameters are maximized. The track is then fit at the resolved values, so the hyperparameters' own uncertainty does not enter the fitted values.

The search is a Nelder–Mead simplex (_map_ou_hypers), stopped once the negative log posterior agrees across the simplex to $10^{-8}$ nats, which places the optimum within about $10^{-4}$ of its own standard error: an optimum resolved only relative to the objective's size would wander between calls by a sizable fraction of its standard error on long tracks. $\tau$ is confined to between one median sample spacing and ten times the span of the samples (for pooled tracks, the widest such range any one track allows), since outside it the data cannot distinguish values of $\tau$. The search for $\sigma^2$ starts from the tracks' weighted variance less the expected noise contribution, and is floored at $10^{-8}$.

The hyperpriors must be proper. With a flat hyperprior, the likelihood alone drives $\sigma \to 0$ on a track whose structure is below the noise, so a weak track would be fit as a constant regardless of what it is. A proper hyperprior on $\sigma$ keeps it at a plausible value. The adhoc default, default_adhoc_prior, puts LogNormal hyperpriors on $\tau$ (median 10 s) and $\sigma$ (median 0.3 rad), each with log-standard deviation 1.

The random-walk prior

A RandomWalkPrior(; order = m, σ) is the $(m-1)$-times integrated Brownian motion along the coordinate. Its state at each sample is $s_k = (f_k, f'_k, \dots, f^{(m-1)}_k)$, and over a step $\Delta_k = |x_k - x_{k-1}|$ it evolves as

\[s_k = A_k s_{k-1} + \eta_k, \qquad (A_k)_{ij} = \frac{\Delta_k^{\,j-i}}{(j-i)!}\ (j \ge i), \qquad (Q_k)_{ij} = \frac{\sigma^2\, \Delta_k^{\,2m-1-i-j}}{(2m-1-i-j)\,(m-1-i)!\,(m-1-j)!},\]

with $\eta_k \sim \mathcal N(0, Q_k)$ and indices $i, j$ from 0. For $m = 1$ this is $f_k - f_{k-1} \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2 \Delta_k)$; for $m = 2$ the posterior mean is a cubic smoothing spline. $\sigma^2$ is per unit of $x^{2m-1}$. Because the prior is defined in the coordinate, not by counting samples, it holds for uneven spacing and gaps and keeps its meaning when the segments are made coarser or finer.

The starting value and its first $m - 1$ derivatives are left free (a flat start), so the prior is improper in those directions (RandomWalkModel). The same Kalman filter and RTS smoother fit it. Until the samples determine the state, the filter carries it in information form, the density $\exp(c + \eta^\top s - \tfrac12 s^\top \Lambda s)$ of the samples so far and the state, starting from $\Lambda = 0$, $\eta = 0$, $c = 0$. A sample with data adds $e_1 e_1^\top / r_k$ to $\Lambda$, $e_1 y_k / r_k$ to $\eta$ and $-\tfrac12[\log(2\pi r_k) + y_k^2/r_k]$ to $c$; a step, with $M = A_k^{-\top} \Lambda A_k^{-1}$, $\tilde\eta = A_k^{-\top}\eta$ and $F = I + Q_k M$, sets

\[\Lambda \leftarrow F^{-\top} M, \qquad \eta \leftarrow F^{-\top} \tilde\eta, \qquad c \leftarrow c + \tfrac12 \tilde\eta^\top Q_k F^{-\top} \tilde\eta - \log\lvert\det A_k\rvert - \tfrac12 \log\det F,\]

which needs no $Q_k^{-1}$. Once $\Lambda$ is positive definite, after $m$ samples with data, the filter continues in covariance form from $P = \Lambda^{-1}$, $\mu = P\eta$. This is exact, not a large finite starting variance. The smoother runs back through those first samples with the conditional of $s_k$ given $s_{k+1}$ in the same form.

The likelihood with the flat start integrated out (the restricted likelihood, as for the level above) is $c + \tfrac12 \eta^\top \Lambda^{-1} \eta - \tfrac12 \log\det\Lambda + \tfrac m2 \log 2\pi$ at the switch, plus the usual innovation terms after it (_random_walk_loglik). It is taken under the Lebesgue measure on the first sample's state, derivatives in units of the coordinate. The coordinate is first shifted and rescaled by its median spacing so the states have comparable magnitudes, and the fit is in at least Float64. A track with fewer than $m$ samples with data does not determine the walk and keeps its measured values. Since a walk with a flat start leaves its own level free, it cannot be separated from a level component, and such a walk beside a level is rejected.

An init gives the first sample's state a Gaussian prior $s_1 \sim \mathcal N(\mu_0, P_0)$ instead, in units of the coordinate and relative to the track's level, and the walk is then proper. The filter starts in covariance form from it, exactly as for OU, and the likelihood is the ordinary one. A level beside it is estimated, and integrated out for the hyperparameters, as for OU above, so the init's spread describes how far the track's initial state may lie from its level.

σ may be a hyperprior, resolved by the same type-II MAP as an OU prior's, maximizing

\[\sum_{\text{tracks}} \log p_R(y \mid \sigma) + \log \pi_\sigma(\sigma) + \log\sigma\]

over $\log\sigma$, where $p_R$ is the restricted likelihood above. The search starts from the variance of the $m$-th differences of the samples with data, less their noise contribution, set equal to the variance a walk gives an $m$-th difference at the median spacing $h$, $c_m \sigma^2 h^{2m-1}$ ($c_1 = 1$, $c_2 = 2/3$). There are no search bounds, and the hyperprior must be proper: as $\sigma \to 0$ the restricted likelihood tends to that of a polynomial of degree $m - 1$ fit to the samples, which stays finite, so with a flat hyperprior a track whose structure is below the noise would be fit as a polynomial. The solvers pool tracks as they do for an OU prior. A walk with a flat start has no separate level, so no levels are integrated out; with an init they are, as for OU (_estimate_hypers).

The CARMA prior

A CARMAPrior(; ar, ma, σ) is the stationary continuous-time autoregressive moving-average process of Kelly et al. (2014), CARMA($p, q$), along the coordinate:

\[a(D)\, x = b(D)\, \varepsilon, \qquad a(s) = \prod_{k=1}^{p} (s - r_k), \qquad b(s) = \prod_{k=1}^{q} \Big(1 - \frac{s}{\rho_k}\Big),\]

with $D$ the derivative along the coordinate and $\varepsilon$ white noise. Each root is given as a factor: a RealRoot(; scale) is $-1/\text{scale}$, and a ComplexRoots(; period, scale) the pair $-1/\text{scale} \pm 2\pi i/\text{period}$, so every root has a negative real part and the process is stationary for any positive values. $\sigma$ is its stationary standard deviation. The power spectrum is $\propto |b(2\pi i\varphi)|^2 / |a(2\pi i\varphi)|^2$ at frequency $\varphi$ along the coordinate. A real autoregressive root adds an exponential decay to the autocovariance, and a complex pair a term $\propto e^{-|\Delta|/\text{scale}} \cos(2\pi\Delta/\text{period} + \vartheta)$, a ripple whose phase stays coherent over about scale; as scale grows it tends to a sinusoid of fixed period with Gaussian amplitudes. Moving-average roots raise the spectrum's tail: the process is $p - q - 1$ times differentiable, so $q = p - 1$ keeps the channel-to-channel roughness of an OU process beside the autoregressive structure. With distinct roots the autocovariance is $\sum_k b(r_k)\, b(-r_k)\, e^{r_k |\Delta|} / \big(a'(r_k)\, a(-r_k)\big)$, up to scale.

The state is the observer canonical one (CARMAModel), whose first entry is $x$: $s' = F s + g \varepsilon$ with $-a_1, \dots, -a_p$ down the first column of $F$ and ones above its diagonal, ``a(s) = s^p + a_1 s^{p-1} + \dots

  • ap$, and$gk$the coefficient of$s^{p-k}$in$b``. Over a step

$\Delta$, $A = e^{F\Delta}$ and $Q = \int_0^\Delta e^{Ft} g g^\top e^{F^\top t}\, dt$ come from one matrix exponential (Van Loan 1978), computed once per distinct step. The start is the stationary covariance, from the Lyapunov equation $F P + P F^\top + g g^\top = 0$, solved after rescaling the coordinate by the roots' geometric mean magnitude, where $F$ is of order one. As for a random walk, the coordinate is first rescaled by its median spacing, so a step is of order one. A step's $Q$ is still nearly singular where the roots are slow against it, $|r_k|\Delta \ll 1$: the state then evolves almost deterministically over the step, and $Q^{-1}$ is large along the directions it barely moves. The MAP therefore takes the prior energy and its gradient from the whitened innovations $L^{-1}(s_k - A s_{k-1})$, $Q = L L^\top$, rather than from the assembled quadratic, whose entries carry the rounding of $Q^{-1}$. The prior is proper, so a level beside it is estimated, and integrated out for the hyperparameters, as for OU, and every hyperparameter given as a density is estimated by the same type-II MAP over the logs. The hyperprior is restricted to the hyperparameters the samples measure: a scale between the smallest median sample spacing $\Delta$ and the largest piece's span $W$, and a period between $2\Delta$ and $W$, below which it aliases to a longer one. The search starts from the scales and periods spread evenly in log over $[\Delta, W]$.

Phase tracks and their 2π branches

A phase is measured from a complex gain, so each sample's value $\theta_k$ is known only modulo $2\pi$. The likelihood does not depend on the branch, but a prior on the real-valued track does: moving one sample by $2\pi$ leaves the data's fit unchanged and adds a step the prior penalizes. Fitting a phase track under a prior therefore means choosing an integer $m_k$ for each sample as well, the track being observed as

\[\theta_k + 2\pi m_k = L + f_k + \varepsilon_k .\]

Choosing $m_k$ by stepping from each sample to its neighbor (nearest-branch unwrapping, unwrap_phase_track) works while the noise on a step stays well inside $\pm\pi$. At lower signal-to-noise ratio one noisy step puts every later sample a turn off; the smoother then fits that step as a smooth ramp, and an iterative solve that refines each sample about the current fit keeps it, since removing it means moving many samples by $2\pi$ at once. The branches must be chosen under the prior, not before it.

A sum of Gaussians per sample

If the lifted phase has Gaussian noise of variance $r_k$, the likelihood of the wrapped sample is the wrapped normal, a sum over branches:

\[p(\theta_k \mid f_k) = \sum_{m \in \mathbb Z} \mathcal N\!\big(\theta_k + 2\pi m;\; L + f_k,\; r_k\big).\]

Under a state-space prior, the posterior over the state and a branch history $m_{1:k}$ is then a mixture with one Kalman filter per history, each weighted by the marginal likelihood of its lifted samples (a Gaussian-sum filter; Sorenson and Alspach 1971; Alspach and Sorenson 1972). At each sample with data, every history $h$ branches into one child per $m$: the child updates $h$'s filter with the lifted value $\theta_k + 2\pi m$ and multiplies its weight by the innovation density,

\[w_{h,m} = w_h\, \mathcal N\!\big(\theta_k + 2\pi m - (\mu^-_{h,k})_1;\; 0,\; S_k\big).\]

The predicted covariance, gain and innovation variance do not depend on the samples' values, so all histories share them; a history is its state mean, its log weight and its branches. Summed over every history, the weights give the marginal likelihood of the wrapped track, $p(\theta) = \sum_h w_h$, and the most probable history gives the branches the track is fit on (_branch_filter).

The sum over histories grows geometrically, so it is truncated as in multiple-hypothesis tracking (Reid 1979):

  • a history branches only to the values within 5 predictive standard deviations $\sqrt{S_k}$ of its prediction, and always to the nearest; a farther branch's density is below $e^{-12.5}$ of the peak;
  • a history whose log weight falls 25 below the best one's is dropped, a relative weight below $10^{-10}$;
  • at most 64 histories are carried;
  • two histories whose predicted states agree to $10^{-3}$ of the state's standard deviations have the same future, so they are merged: their weights are summed, which keeps the marginal likelihood, and the more probable one's branches are kept, as in the Viterbi algorithm (Viterbi 1967).

At high signal-to-noise ratio only the nearest branch is within reach of each prediction and one history is carried; the filter is then the ordinary one on the lifted track. Histories multiply only where a sample's branch is in doubt.

A flat start. A random walk with a flat start has no prediction until its first $m$ samples with data fix the state, and every branch of those samples is equally probable. Each takes the branch nearest the previous one. For samples on consecutive points of an evenly spaced grid this loses nothing: any other choice differs by a polynomial of degree below $m$ whose values at the grid points are multiples of $2\pi$, which leaves every sample's phase unchanged and which the flat start absorbs. A track whose first samples have gaps between them is restricted to that one choice.

A mean. With a mean beside the prior, its coefficients are integrated out under a flat prior as in The mean above. Each history carries its own running $b$, and $C$ is shared, so a history's prediction of sample $k$ with the mean integrated out is $(\mu^-_{h,k})_1 + (v^H_k)^\top C^{-1} b_h$ with variance $S_k + (v^H_k)^\top C^{-1} v^H_k$, and its weight is $\log w_h = \ell_h + \tfrac12 b_h^\top C^{-1} b_h - \tfrac12\log\det C$. Until the samples determine every coefficient the prediction is flat along the ones they leave free, so each sample takes its nearest branch, which the mean absorbs: one sample for a level, two for a level and a delay. Each track is filtered with its own coefficients. Tracks that share a coefficient are then aligned in turn: each is moved by the multiple of $2\pi$ that brings its weighted mean nearest the mean predicted there by a weighted least-squares fit of the tracks before it, so a delay shared across spectral windows carries the phase from one window to the next.

A track whose level is free, under a flat start or beside a level, is finally moved by the multiple of $2\pi$ that keeps it nearest its input, so an iterative solve does not see it jump by whole turns between iterations.

The hyperparameters over every branch

A phase track's hyperparameters can be estimated by the type-II MAP above with the likelihood summed over the branch histories, $\log \sum_h w_h$, in place of the likelihood of one lifted track. Where several tracks share a mean, the pooled restricted likelihood of their most probable lifts is used, plus, for each track, the log of its histories' total weight relative to its most probable one's (_branch_pooled_loglik); without a shared mean that is exactly the summed likelihood. Each evaluation of the objective runs the filter again at the trial hyperparameters, so the branches and the hyperparameters are estimated together. The random walk's search starts from the $m$-th differences reduced modulo $2\pi$, which their integer coefficients make well defined. The bandpass solve uses this for the track alone to start its hyperparameters, then fits them by EM on the chosen branches together with every other track's (see The bandpass posterior).

Limits

The wrapped normal is the likelihood when the phase noise is Gaussian. A phase derived from a complex measurement has non-Gaussian noise at low signal-to-noise ratio; treating it as Gaussian with the weight's variance is the same linearization the solvers already make. The filter chooses the most probable branch history, which need not be the branches of the single most probable track, and the truncation drops histories whose total weight is small by construction.

The same structure, an integer per observation estimated alongside a real-valued state, is the carrier-phase ambiguity problem of satellite navigation, where the integers are resolved by integer least squares (Teunissen 1995). There the integers enter a general least-squares problem; here the Markov prior makes the search sequential. Kalman filtering has also been used to unwrap interferometric phase in radar imaging (Loffeld et al. 2008).

The bandpass posterior

solve_joint_bandpass! fits the complex visibilities against $V_{ab}(\nu) \approx g_a(\nu)\, S\, \overline{g_b(\nu)}$, with one complex gain $g = e^{\ell + i\phi}$ per (station, feed, frequency segment, time segment) slot and one frequency-flat source term $S_J$ per $J$ = (scan, station pair, feed pair). Its objective is the negative log posterior

\[O(\ell, \phi) = \tfrac12 \chi^2\big(g, \hat S(g)\big) + \sum_{\text{tracks}} E(\ell_{\text{track}}) + \sum_{\text{tracks}} E(\phi_{\text{track}}),\]

with $\hat S(g)$ the closed-form least-squares source term for the gains and $E$ each track's prior energy. With $u_c = g_a \bar g_b$ over the cells $c$ of $J$ and $r_c$ their weighted sums, profiling $S$ gives

\[\chi^2_J(g) = \sum_c \frac{|r_c|^2}{w_c} - \frac{\big|\sum_c \bar u_c r_c\big|^2}{\sum_c w_c |u_c|^2}.\]

What the data leave free

Two kinds of phase shift leave every $\chi^2_J$ unchanged:

  • The gauge. A phase added to every slot of a connected component of the correlation graph cancels from $g_a S \bar g_b$ on every correlation in it. Where the stations share one segmentation, the components are the array's (frequency segment, time segment) cells, so the shift is a phase spectrum common to every station.
  • The band constants. $S_J$ is frequency-flat, so a constant $\alpha_t$ added to every slot of one (station, feed, time segment) track $t$ is absorbed by the $S_J$ of its correlations.

A proper prior along frequency sees a common spectrum, except the part its track's mean takes up: a constant under a level, and a line in frequency under a delay. Let $F$ be the common shifts every prior is blind to. That is one constant per set of components the priors join, plus a line under a delay, and one constant per component where no prior acts. A shift $f \in F$, with track constants $\alpha$, moves slot $n$ of track $t$ by

\[\delta\phi_n = f_n + \alpha_t,\]

which neither the data nor the priors see. One constant on every slot, with every $\alpha_t$ its negative, moves nothing, so a connected set of $T$ tracks has $|F| + T - 1$ such directions. The MAP is unique only when the solve fixes each of them, and nothing more. It does so in two parts (_joint_bandpass_gauge!):

  1. Each track holds its band constant. Under a proper prior acting on the track, the track's level is held at zero, and the prior sets the constant through the mean it is profiled over. Any other track holds its best-measured slot at zero. A hold sets $\alpha_t = -\ell_t(f)$, with $\ell_t(f)$ the held level's share of $f$ or $f$ at the held slot, and leaves $f_n - \ell_t(f)$.
  2. The gauge pins what the holds leave. Within a set of components that the priors join, a pivoted QR of $f_n - \ell_t(f)$, weighted by each slot's data, picks the pins. Across those sets, the pins tie each track's constant to every set it reaches, best-measured slots first, so that the pins and holds form a spanning forest over the tracks and sets. That makes $|F| - 1$ pins per connected set: none under a level alone, one (for the line) under a delay, and one per component but one where no prior acts.

After the MAP, a shift along $F$ meets the gauge asked for; under ZeroSumPhase, that is a zero mean over the slots along each freedom. Neither the data nor the priors see the shift, so the pins choose only the solve's path, not its result.

PinAntenna pins more than $F$: the reference's slot in every component. The reference's bandpass is zero by definition, the other tracks' priors act on their bandpasses relative to it, and the reference's track, fixed throughout, takes no part in the hyperparameter fit. A track is then held only where its pins leave its constant free, so the reference holds nothing. Where the reference has no data in a component, the next reference with data is pinned there instead (gauge_anchor). Whether that station also holds its constant depends on the priors:

  • With a prior, the prior sees a jump in the common phase at that component, so the pin alone fixes the station's constant. Holding its level as well would pull its bandpass toward a zero mean that nothing measures.
  • Without a prior, nothing ties the common phase in that component to the other components. The pin fixes only the sum of that phase and the station's constant, so the station also holds a slot in a component the reference pins. Without that hold, the common phase in the component would be undetermined, and the published phases there would depend on where the solve started.

Any direction left unfixed makes the MAP objective flat along it: the curvature is singular there, and the solution along that direction is whatever the start gives.

The prior as a sparse quadratic

A state-space prior's energy over a track, written over its states rather than through the filter, is a sum of small quadratic terms: for each step between samples, $\tfrac12 (s_k - A_k s_{k-1})^\top Q_k^{-1} (s_k - A_k s_{k-1})$, and for a proper start $\tfrac12 (s_1 - \mu_0)^\top P_0^{-1} (s_1 - \mu_0)$, where the first entry of $s_k$ is the track's value less its mean. Minimized over the latent entries of the states (the derivatives of a random walk) and the mean's coefficients, this is the energy the Kalman filter gives with the values observed exactly, and a flat start is exact: no start term at all, rather than a large starting variance (_track_energy). The unknowns of one track are its values, its latent states and its mean's coefficients, and the terms couple only neighboring samples, so the energy of every track is one sparse quadratic. This is the smoothing problem written as least squares, which the RTS smoother solves by elimination along the chain (Bell 1994).

The source term is frequency-flat, so it absorbs any constant added to a track: $\chi^2_J$ is unchanged by it, and the data cannot measure a track's band constant. Where a proper prior (an OU prior, or a random walk with an init) acts on the track, the solve holds the track's first level at zero and lets the prior set the constant: the slots are free to move together, so the MAP of everything else is that under the prior profiled over the constant. On a track no such prior acts on, one slot of the track is held.

The step

The objective's gradient is the held-$S$ gradient at $\hat S$, since $\hat S$ is stationary. In one observable's slot values $x$ ($\phi$, or $\ell$), each cell sees $x_a + \sigma x_b$ with $\sigma = -1$ for the phase and $+1$ for the log-amplitude, and the two observables decouple. With $z_c = \bar u_c r_c$, $p_c = w_c |u_c|^2$, $D = \sum_c p_c$ and $S = \sum_c z_c / D$, the Hessian of $\chi^2_J/2$ over the cells is, for the phase,

\[\operatorname{diag}\big(\operatorname{Re}(\bar S z_c)\big) - \operatorname{Re}(z z^{\mathsf H})/D,\]

and for the log-amplitude

\[\operatorname{diag}\big(2|S|^2 p_c - \operatorname{Re}(\bar S z_c)\big) - \operatorname{Re}(z z^{\mathsf H})/D + 2\,(t p^\top + p t^\top)/D - 4 |S|^2 p p^\top / D, \qquad t = \operatorname{Re}(\bar S z).\]

Where the model fits the data, $z = S p$, and both reduce to the Gauss–Newton curvature with $S$ profiled: the held-$S$ curvature $q_c = |S|^2 p_c$ less its Schur complement in $S$, $q q^\top / \sum_c q_c$. Where the data are weak, $|\hat S|^2$ holds the noise as well as the signal, and the Gauss–Newton form overstates the curvature of a profiled χ² that is nearly flat: along the array-wide R–L spectrum, which only the cross-hands measure, by an order of magnitude on real data, so Gauss–Newton steps there crawl. The solve takes Gauss–Newton steps while they are large, which keeps them in the basin they start in, and the exact Hessian once a step moves no gain by more than one standard error, or by fewer than ten while the objective falls by more than 1.5 times what the Gauss–Newton model predicts (_profile_product!, _gauss_newton_product!).

Either curvature is dense across frequency, since each $J$ couples every cell of its two tracks, but a product with it is one pass over the cells: the held-$S$ part is sparse and the rest has rank at most three per $J$. Each step solves the system of the Hessian, the exact prior quadratic and a damping $\lambda/2\,\lVert x' - x \rVert^2$ over the slots, over the slots, latent states and means, by preconditioned conjugate gradients (LinearSolve.jl's KrylovJL_CG, stopping at nonpositive curvature). The preconditioner (ProfilePreconditioner) is the sparse Cholesky factor of the held-$S$ system, ordered by frequency so that it is banded, balanced on the coarse space of the track constants (Mandel 1993): the held-$S$ curvature sees a track's constant through every correlation of the track, the profiled one only through its prior, and on that space the preconditioner takes the profiled curvature, which there is the prior's alone. A step is accepted when it does not raise the objective, with $S$ re-solved, and $\lambda$, a multiple $\mu$ of the largest data curvature, follows the ratio of the objective's actual decrease to the model's predicted one (Madsen, Nielsen & Tingleff 2004).

The solve starts each slot at its own gain estimate given every other gain and $S$, repeated with $S$ re-solved (Salvini & Wijnholds 2014). A phase spectrum $\rho(\nu)$ added to one feed of every station leaves every same-feed product unchanged and turns each product pairing that feed with another by $\pm\rho$. Only those products measure it, and the estimates the rounds take are led by the same-feed products, so the rounds leave it where it began; a delay there of $1/B$ across a band $B$ winds those products a full turn, and the MAP would start where their χ² is concave in $\rho$. Each feed after the first is therefore searched for one delay common to every station, $\rho = 2\pi\tau(\nu - \bar\nu)$, against the products pairing it with an earlier feed: with $S$ profiled, a product group $J$ lowers the χ² by $|\sum_c e^{\mp i\rho(\nu_c)} \bar u_c r_c|^2 / \sum_c w_c |u_c|^2$, which on noise alone is χ² with two degrees of freedom. The delay is applied when the best trial's false-alarm probability over the grid is below $10^{-4}$, and the solve warns otherwise. The search works on feed indices, so it is the same for circular, linear and mixed feeds. The MAP stops when the undamped step would move no gain by more than tolerance of its standard error from the data. The latent states are unknowns of the MAP beside the gains: they start at their optimum given the gains and move with each step, so the prior energy compared between steps is a sum of whitened innovations at given states, free of the rounding of a solve as stiff as the stiffest prior. Each step's Krylov solve starts at the current point, so its initial residual is the gradient and its stopping rule, a tenth of that residual, bounds the step's error relative to the step. Where a slot's phase is held more than $\pi/2$ from its own data, by a phase prior or the gauge, the amplitude that fits it best is zero; without an amplitude prior the posterior then has no mode, and the solve warns.

Hyperparameters

A hyperparameter $\psi$ given as a density is estimated by MAP-EM: the maximum of the hyperprior density times the marginal likelihood of the data, the gains integrated out, by the EM algorithm (Dempster, Laird & Rubin 1977) with the posterior of the gains at given $\psi$ approximated by a Gaussian at its mode (Laplace; Tierney & Kadane 1986). Each EM map

  1. finds the MAP of the gains under the current $\psi$ (the step above);
  2. takes, for each track, the posterior means and second moments of its state chain, $v_k = (s_k, s_{k-1})$, under the Gaussian whose precision is the preconditioner's curvature: held-$S$, except on the track constants, which keep their prior variance;
  3. maximizes, for each track, its expected log prior density plus its hyperprior over the log hyperparameters, within the hyperprior's support,

\[\psi' = \arg\max_\psi\; \log p(\psi) + \sum_k \Big(-\tfrac12 \log\det(2\pi Q_k) - \tfrac12 \operatorname{tr}\big(Q_k^{-1} G_k\, \mathrm{E}[v_k v_k^\top]\, G_k^\top\big)\Big), \qquad G_k = [I, -A_k],\]

a two-parameter search for an OU prior. A CARMA prior's latent states depend on its roots, so the moments are taken in the basis of the current $\psi$ and the expected log prior evaluated in that of $\psi'$. EM holds all the same: for any family of joint densities $f_\psi(y, s)$ over the same $s$ that integrate to the marginal likelihood, Jensen's inequality gives $\log \int f_{\psi'} \ge \mathrm{E}_\psi[\log f_{\psi'} - \log f_\psi] + \log \int f_\psi$, and the data enter only through the first entry, which is the same in every basis. The second moments need only the covariances of unknowns that share a term, which lie on the sparsity pattern of the system and so of its Cholesky factor; the recursion of Takahashi, Fagan & Chen (1973), in its supernodal form (SelectedInversion.jl), gives those entries of the inverse without forming the rest. The expected log prior is linear in the moments, so they are summed once per distinct step before the search, and each evaluation in it costs one transition per distinct step, not one per sample.

The E-step's expectation carries the posterior's spread, so EM does not collapse a prior onto its mode as a joint MAP over the gains and $\psi$ would, where $-\tfrac12 \log\det Q_k$ grows without bound as $\sigma \to 0$. EM converges linearly, slowly where the data barely determine a direction, as for the array-wide R–L spectrum, so the maps are accelerated by SQUAREM (Varadhan & Roland 2008, scheme S3) over the log hyperparameters. EM stops once a SQUAREM cycle moves no gain by more than tolerance of its standard error: the gains are what the solve is for, and the hyperparameters they no longer depend on, which the data barely measure, would take EM many more cycles. The hyperparameters start from type-II MAP on each track alone, taking each slot's own estimate given every other gain and $S$ as an independent measurement; for a phase track that start is summed over the branch histories.

Branches

The phase branches are chosen once, from the slot estimates at the start under the starting hyperparameters (see Phase tracks and their 2π branches). The data are fringe-corrected before the bandpass, so phase steps along frequency are small, and the MAP and EM then move the phases continuously on those branches.

Where the solvers use this

Bandpass, JointSmoother. Each (station, feed, time segment) has one track per spectral window along frequency, and the solve maximizes the posterior of every track at once, with the per-scan source term profiled out, as described in The bandpass posterior above. Each phase window is put on its most probable branches once, at the start, and the hyperparameters are fit by MAP-EM around the MAP solve.

Adhoc phase, PerTrackAdhocSmoother. After the per-AP solve, each (station, feed node) track of a scan is fit on its own: hyperparameters per (station, node, scan) with the level integrated out, then the level plus the MAP of the zero-mean part. The per-scan mean is then removed from every track, since a per-station constant trades against the baseline source terms.

Adhoc phase, JointKalmanSmoother. The state stacks every station's state under its own prior: its phase $\theta_i$ for an OU prior, or $(\theta_i, \theta_i', \dots)$ for a random walk with an init. The stations are independent, so the transition is block diagonal, one block per station. Each gated (baseline, correlation product) cell at an AP is an observation

\[y_{ab} = \theta_a - \theta_b + \varepsilon_{ab}, \qquad \varepsilon_{ab} \sim \mathcal N\!\big(0, 1/\mathrm{SNR}_{ab}^2\big),\]

with its source term already removed. The same filter and smoother, in matrix form (kalman_mv_filter, rts_smooth_mv), give the posterior mean of every station's track from the baseline data directly, without first solving each AP. Differences leave the sum of all station phases unconstrained; only the priors hold it near zero, which is why every station needs a proper prior (an OU prior or a walk with an init), and the result is re-referenced to the anchor station at every AP afterwards. Each observed phase is moved by a multiple of 2π toward the current model before the filter runs, and this is repeated a few times as the model improves.

Each station's hyperparameters are resolved by type-II MAP on its track from the per-AP solve, with the level integrated out, and the track is centered on its level for the filter. The anchor's per-AP track is zero by construction, and an unobserved station has none, so both take the median of the resolved values of the stations whose prior has the same form (OU, or a walk of the same order), unless their own are fixed.

In both adhoc solvers, the complex-domain refinement (see AdhocOptions) repeats the fit on observations linearized around the current tracks, re-resolving the hyperparameters each pass.
